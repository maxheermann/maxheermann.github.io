  • Heermann, M., Steinecke, D. & Leuffen, D. Compensation or Contagion? Public Support for International Cooperation after American Hegemony. Under review at International Organization.

    Abstract

    International Relations theory has long debated how international orders are sustained. We use the recent dramatic shifts in US foreign policy to empirically inquire what happens when a hegemon turns away from contributing to the production of international collective goods. Do other states respond by compensating for the loss, or do they fall prey to negative contagion effects? Assuming that public support is crucial for a sustained provision of costly measures in democracies, we analyze how citizens in other liberal democracies react to US withdrawals from multilateral cooperation. We argue that public support for compensation is higher (a) in policy areas with more tangible national self-interest, (b) in cases where the hegemon contributed less, and (c) when a coalition of like-minded states announces a joint response. Empirically, we present results from two survey experiments conducted in Canada, France, Germany and Japan, close allies of the US and traditional supporters of the liberal international order. Our findings reveal that US exits from international organizations (IO) reduce public support for IO membership, in line with the contagion hypothesis. At the same time, IO supporters increase their willingness to step up and contribute more to IO budgets. Collective initiatives by coalitions of like-minded countries and appeals emphasizing the humanitarian and geopolitical costs of withdrawal increase support for more international engagement. In times of the politicization of international cooperation, our findings provide insights into the resilience of public support for the LIO and the need for political leadership to safeguard international collective goods.

  • Pasquettaz, G., Heermann, M. & Leuffen, D. The Populist Contagion: How the Mainstreaming of Right-Wing Populist Rhetoric Reduces Public Support for International Aid. Revise & resubmit at Journal of European Public Policy.

    Abstract

    This article examines whether the diffusion of populist rhetoric from populist to mainstream parties reduces public support for international organisations (IOs) and multilateral aid. We argue that when mainstream parties adopt of populist anti-IO rhetoric, support for aid decreases not just amongst citizens, who hold populist attitudes and support populist parties, but also amongst more moderate voters. To test this cueing argument, we ran a survey experiment in France, Germany, Poland, Spain, and Sweden (n = 10,513) in early 2026. Varying both the message and the messenger, the experiment reveals, first, that populist anti-IO cues, indeed, reduce support for multilateral aid. Second, the messenger matters – while populist cues from populist parties primarily affect ideologically like-minded citizens, populist rhetoric voiced by mainstream parties reduces support across the political spectrum. Third, anti-IO rhetoric specifically undermines multilateral forms of aid while leaving support for bilateral aid unchanged. In sum, our study demonstrates how the normalisation of populist rhetoric by mainstream parties jeopardises public support for multilateral responses to transboundary risks and crises. Moreover, our findings underline that the populist contestation of IOs and multilateral aid is conceptually distinct from deservingness perceptions of the global poor.

  • Heermann, M., Baute, S. & Leuffen, D. Public Support for Medical Patent Waivers: Evidence from a Cross-National Survey. Revise & resubmit at Acta Politica.

    Abstract

    Medical patents are a contentious issue in global health policy. While advocates argue that patents incentivise scientific and medical innovation, critics contend that they restrict access to essential medicines and vaccines, exacerbating global health inequalities. Despite the political sensitivity of this debate, little is known about citizens’ attitudes towards medical patents and proposals to abolish them. We address this research gap by analysing novel survey data from nine countries across five continents. Whereas the governments of high-income countries and those of middle- and low-income countries often take opposing stances on patent waivers, we find no clear Global North-South divide in public opinion. Instead, our data reveals that public opinion on patent waivers is divided within countries and reflects prevailing policy discourses about international solidarity, economic growth and scientific innovation. Citizens with altruistic, cosmopolitan, and post-materialist values are more likely to support patent waivers. Conversely, citizens who prioritise economic growth, support merit-based rewards, and express high trust in science are more opposed to patent waivers. These findings provide new insights into the political foundations of public support for global health policies.

  • Heermann, M. Of Activists and Platforms: Digital Rights Activism, Business Power and Political Alignments in the Digital Economy.

    [ECPR Standing Group on Interest Groups: Annual Best Paper Award 2024, Honorable Mention]

    Abstract

    Under what conditions do business and civil society groups form heterogeneous lobbying coalitions? While previous research has investigated the conditions for successful cooperation between these “strange bedfellows”, there has been less work on when and why their policy preferences align in the first place. This article studies this question in the case of the digital economy, focusing on the relationship between platform companies and digital rights activists. It argues that intermediary regulation shapes patterns of interest group contestation: When intermediary regulation seeks to constrain citizens’ online freedoms, the preferences of digital rights groups and platform companies align. Conversely, when intermediary regulation seeks to limit the discretionary power of platforms or of other intermediaries to empower citizens and platform-dependent businesses, the preferences of activists and intermediaries diverge. The results of a discourse network analysis of four European Union stakeholder consultations in the areas of data protection, online content, and Internet traffic management support the theoretical argument. By analyzing under what conditions digital rights groups and platform companies share preference regarding the regulation of digital markets, the article contributes to our understanding of the political cleavages shaping digital capitalism.

  • Baute, S., Heermann, M, Leuffen, D. & Pasquettaz, G. Mind the Populist Gap: How Multilateralists Can Defend Global Health Governance.

    Abstract

    Background: Global health governance is increasingly challenged by populist leaders who frame international organizations like the WHO as antidemocratic threats. These leaders claim to speak for the people, but their stance may not reflect broader public preferences.
    Methods: To test this, we conducted a survey of 13,992 citizens across nine countries on support for health cooperation, global health funds, and trust in the WHO.
    Results: In all countries, a clear majority favored increased international health cooperation and financial contributions, and moderately high trust in the WHO prevailed.
    Conclusions: Contrary to populist rhetoric, most citizens back multilateral health action; policymakers should seize this ‘populist gap’ by offering strong political leadership that emphasizes long term collective benefits.
